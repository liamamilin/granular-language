17  Evaluation

Language does not only describe. It also judges.

A person may say that a tool is sharp, but also that it is useful. One may say that a room is crowded, but also that the arrangement is inefficient. A decision may be called risky, but also unfair. A meal may be warm, but also good or bad. In ordinary life, people do not remain at the level of neutral description for long. They move from what is the case to what they think of it.

This is why evaluation matters.

Granular language becomes more mature when it can distinguish clearly between description and judgment. A statement such as the bag is heavy describes a condition. A statement such as the bag is too heavy begins to evaluate it. A statement such as the plan is long describes duration. A statement such as the plan is inefficient evaluates performance against a standard. Higher semantic control requires knowing when language is merely stating and when it is also judging.

17.1 What is evaluation?

At the level of ordinary language, evaluation is the act of judging something as good or bad, useful or harmful, fair or unfair, efficient or inefficient, or by some other value-bearing standard.

We notice evaluation through simple patterns such as:

  • good and bad

  • useful and harmful

  • fair and unfair

  • efficient and inefficient

  • distinctions between description and judgment

Examples include:

  • a good tool

  • a bad result

  • a useful habit

  • harmful behavior

  • a fair decision

  • an inefficient process

Evaluation is not only about what something is.
It is about how it stands relative to a standard.

17.2 Why evaluation matters

Evaluation matters because human language is not neutral all the way through.

We need to know:

  • when a word is describing and when it is judging

  • what standards an evaluation depends on

  • how evaluative terms add a new layer of meaning

  • why precision improves when evaluation is separated from bare description

Without this, language becomes confused in two common ways:

  • description may be mistaken for neutral fact when it already includes judgment

  • judgment may be treated as fact without showing the standard behind it

Granular language improves when it can say clearly: this is description; this is evaluation; and this is the criterion being used.

17.3 Key distinctions

17.3.1 1. Good and bad

Some evaluations are broad and general.

Evaluation Description Examples
good judged positively by some standard good meal, good plan, good result
bad judged negatively by some standard bad habit, bad road, bad outcome

Examples:

  • This is a good knife.

  • That was a bad decision.

  • We had a good day.

  • The result was bad.

This matters because good and bad are common, but often too broad unless the standard is clearer.

17.3.2 2. Useful and harmful

Some evaluations focus on practical effect.

Evaluation Description Examples
useful helps achieve a purpose or supports a need useful tool, useful advice, useful habit
harmful causes damage, suffering, or bad effect harmful smoke, harmful behavior, harmful habit

Examples:

  • This tool is useful.

  • Fresh air is useful for recovery.

  • Smoking is harmful to health.

  • Careless language can be harmful.

This matters because usefulness and harm are often more precise than broad good-bad judgments.

17.3.3 3. Fair and unfair

Some evaluations concern justice, balance, or proper treatment.

Evaluation Description Examples
fair judged just, balanced, or reasonable fair rule, fair price, fair treatment
unfair judged unjust, biased, or unreasonable unfair decision, unfair burden, unfair advantage

Examples:

  • The rule is fair.

  • That price seems fair.

  • It is unfair to blame one person for everything.

  • The process was unfair.

This matters because evaluation often includes social standards, not just practical ones.

17.3.4 4. Efficient and inefficient

Some evaluations concern time, effort, cost, or performance.

Evaluation Description Examples
efficient achieves a result with relatively low waste of time, effort, or resources efficient system, efficient method, efficient use of time
inefficient uses more time, effort, or resources than necessary inefficient process, inefficient design, inefficient work pattern

Examples:

  • This method is efficient.

  • The new system works more efficiently.

  • The process is inefficient.

  • Repeating the same mistake is inefficient.

This matters because evaluation often depends on how well something works relative to cost.

17.3.5 5. Evaluation vs. bare description

Description and evaluation are not the same, even when they appear close together.

Bare description Evaluation
The bag is heavy. The bag is too heavy.
The room is small. The room is too small for the group.
The process takes two hours. The process is inefficient.
The rule treats everyone the same. The rule is fair.

Examples:

  • The knife is sharp describes a quality.

  • The knife is good evaluates it.

  • The road is narrow describes a condition.

  • The road is unsafe evaluates risk.

  • The task takes time describes duration.

  • The task is inefficient evaluates performance.

This matters because precision improves when we know whether a sentence is stating or judging.

17.4 Core patterns

This chapter rests on a few basic patterns.

17.4.1 Basic semantic patterns

  • People do not only describe; they also evaluate.

  • Evaluation depends on a standard.

  • Good and bad are broad evaluations.

  • Useful and harmful often focus on practical effect.

  • Fair and unfair often focus on justice or balance.

  • Efficient and inefficient often focus on performance relative to cost.

  • Evaluation differs from bare description.

17.4.2 Basic language patterns

  • ___ is good / bad.

  • ___ is useful / harmful.

  • ___ is fair / unfair.

  • ___ is efficient / inefficient.

  • This is a description, not an evaluation.

  • This judgment depends on the standard of ___.

  • Compared with bare description, this is evaluative language.

17.5 Evaluation and the movement of this volume

Evaluation is a key step in the development of semantic precision.

17.5.1 Earlier chapters refined object, relation, and perspective

  • what is it?

  • what is it like?

  • how does it relate?

  • from whose point of view?

17.5.2 Evaluation now adds judgment

  • is it good or bad?

  • helpful or harmful?

  • fair or unfair?

  • efficient or inefficient?

This is an important shift. Language is no longer only tracking reality and perspective. It is also measuring that reality against standards.

17.6 Examples

Here are some clean examples of evaluative language.

17.6.1 Good and bad

  • This is a good plan.

  • That was a bad choice.

  • The food was good.

  • The result was bad.

17.6.2 Useful and harmful

  • This advice is useful.

  • The tool is useful for careful work.

  • Smoke is harmful.

  • That habit is harmful over time.

17.6.3 Fair and unfair

  • The rule is fair.

  • That treatment was unfair.

  • A fair system builds trust.

  • It is unfair to ignore their effort.

17.6.4 Efficient and inefficient

  • This process is efficient.

  • The new design is more efficient.

  • The system is inefficient.

  • Repeating the task by hand is inefficient.

17.6.5 Evaluation vs. description

  • The room is small.

  • The room is too small for the meeting.

  • The road is long.

  • The route is inefficient.

  • The plan is simple.

  • The plan is better than the last one.

These examples show that evaluative language often includes:

  • standards

  • judgment

  • preference or criterion

  • practical effect

  • social or moral dimension

17.7 Why this matters

To understand evaluation is to understand that precision requires separating what is observed from what is judged.

Without this, language easily becomes mixed and unclear. A person may think they are only describing when they are already judging, or may make a judgment sound like a pure fact. But higher semantic control requires a more disciplined distinction. It asks:

  • what is being described?

  • what is being judged?

  • by what standard?

Evaluation is also essential because many later structures depend on it:

  • argument often depends on evaluative standards

  • disagreement often turns on different evaluations, not different facts

  • context affects what counts as useful, fair, or efficient

  • perspective influences evaluation strongly

  • semantic maturity increases when judgment is made explicit rather than hidden

Granular language therefore requires more than describing the world clearly. It also requires judging it carefully and transparently.

17.8 Chapter summary

This chapter introduces evaluation as the judgmental dimension of language.

People do not only describe reality. They also judge it. Common evaluative patterns include good and bad, useful and harmful, fair and unfair, and efficient and inefficient. Evaluation differs from bare description because it measures something against a standard.

The central lessons of this chapter are:

  • language often includes judgment as well as description

  • evaluation depends on standards

  • good and bad are broad evaluative terms

  • useful and harmful often concern practical effect

  • fair and unfair often concern justice or balance

  • efficient and inefficient often concern performance relative to cost

  • precision improves when evaluation is distinguished from description

To recognize evaluation is to make language more honest and more exact. It reveals not only what is being said about the world, but how the speaker is judging it.
