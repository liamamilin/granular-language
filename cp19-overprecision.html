25  Overprecision

Language is not improved simply by becoming more detailed. Detail can help, but too much detail can also distort.

A description may become so crowded with specification that its main point disappears. A statement may sound exact while resting on weak judgment. A speaker may use numbers, modifiers, and distinctions that add complexity without adding real clarity. In such cases, language does not become more precise. It becomes overprecise. It gives the appearance of control while reducing usefulness, proportion, or truth.

This is why overprecision matters.

After learning how language can increase semantic resolution, we must also learn that refinement has a limit. Precision is valuable when it makes meaning clearer, but not when it adds irrelevant detail, overloads attention, or pretends to know more than is actually known. A mature language system therefore needs a second discipline: not only how to refine, but when to stop refining.

25.1 What is overprecision?

At the level of ordinary language, overprecision is the use of more detail, exactness, or specification than the situation meaningfully requires.

We notice overprecision through simple patterns such as:

  • irrelevant detail

  • cognitive overload

  • false precision

  • situations where simpler language is better

Examples include:

  • giving exact measurements when only rough scale matters

  • adding many qualifiers that do not help the listener

  • using highly specific numbers without reliable evidence

  • explaining a simple act in overly technical language

Overprecision does not mean that precision is bad.
It means that precision has exceeded its useful place.

25.2 Why overprecision matters

Overprecision matters because clarity depends on proportion, not on detail alone.

We need to know:

  • when more detail actually helps

  • when detail becomes irrelevant

  • when exactness becomes misleading

  • when simplicity serves understanding better than narrow specification

Without this understanding, a person may assume that the “most precise” wording is always the best wording. But ordinary language does not work that way. In many cases, a simpler expression is more truthful, more usable, and more cognitively efficient.

Granular language improves not only by becoming finer, but by becoming appropriately fine.

25.3 Key distinctions

25.3.1 1. Irrelevant detail

Some detail adds no useful clarity to the communicative task.

Pattern Description Examples
relevant detail detail that helps understanding, action, or distinction “The bottle contains two liters of water” when planning supply
irrelevant detail detail that adds information without useful function “The bottle contains 2.013 liters” in an ordinary casual context

Examples:

  • We need a large table.
    This may be enough.

  • We need a table exactly 183.4 centimeters long.
    This may be useful only in a technical setting.

  • He walked to the store.

  • He walked to the store in 1,284 small steps.

The second sentence may be more detailed, but not more meaningful for the situation.

This matters because semantic maturity includes knowing which distinctions are worth making.

25.3.2 2. Cognitive overload

Too much precision can burden attention rather than support it.

Pattern Description Examples
manageable detail detail that supports understanding without excess strain short clear instruction, one useful comparison
overload too much detail for the task or listener overqualified explanation, dense numeric clutter, excessive branching

Examples:

  • Add water and boil for ten minutes.

  • Add approximately 247 milliliters of water, heat at this approximate rate, then monitor small changes every thirty seconds.

In some contexts, the second version may be useful. In many ordinary contexts, it creates unnecessary strain.

This matters because language must serve limited human attention.

25.3.3 3. False precision

Sometimes language sounds exact without being genuinely well-grounded.

Pattern Description Examples
real precision exactness supported by reliable measure or strong basis “The meeting lasted 92 minutes” when timed
false precision exact-sounding detail without adequate support “The success rate is 83.742%” without reliable evidence

Examples:

  • He will arrive in about ten minutes.

  • He will arrive in exactly 10.37 minutes.

  • The room felt a little cold.

  • The room was 18.4°C, if that number was never actually measured.

  • The project is likely to take around three months.

  • The project will take exactly 97 days, when no such confidence is justified.

This matters because false precision gives an illusion of knowledge. It can make language sound stronger while making judgment weaker.

25.3.4 4. When simpler language is better

Sometimes a broader statement is more useful, honest, and effective than a narrow one.

Situation Better choice Example
casual everyday speech simpler wording “The room is cold.”
fast practical coordination concise wording “We need more chairs.”
uncertain information approximate wording “The result will come soon.”
high complexity but low need for detail broad summary “The process is inefficient.”

Examples:

  • The child is tired.
    This may be better than a long, uncertain list of possible micro-states.

  • We need more time.
    This may be better than a premature exact schedule.

  • The bag is heavy.
    This may be enough when the goal is simply to warn someone.

This matters because semantic control includes knowing when refinement helps and when it gets in the way.

25.4 Core patterns

This chapter rests on a few basic patterns.

25.4.1 Basic semantic patterns

  • More detail is not always better.

  • Some detail is irrelevant to the communicative purpose.

  • Too much detail can overload attention.

  • Exact-sounding language may be falsely precise.

  • Simpler language is sometimes more appropriate.

  • Precision must be proportionate to task, evidence, and use.

25.4.2 Basic language patterns

  • This level of detail is unnecessary.

  • The extra detail does not help here.

  • The wording creates cognitive overload.

  • This sounds precise, but the precision is false.

  • A simpler description is better in this context.

  • We need enough precision, not maximum precision.

  • The detail should match the use.

25.5 Overprecision and the movement of this volume

Overprecision is a necessary counterweight to the whole project of semantic refinement.

25.5.1 Earlier chapters showed how language becomes more exact

  • names became more specific

  • attributes became more detailed

  • degree became more calibrated

  • measure became more exact

  • relation became more structured

  • context and evaluation became more carefully located

25.5.2 This chapter shows that refinement itself must be governed

  • not every distinction is worth making

  • not every measurable feature needs to be measured

  • not every exact form is more truthful

  • precision must remain subordinate to clarity, relevance, and evidence

This is one of the final lessons of granular language: control is not endless narrowing, but appropriate narrowing.

25.6 Examples

Here are some clean examples of overprecision in use.

25.6.1 Irrelevant detail

  • The chair is near the window.

  • The chair is 47.3 centimeters from the left edge of the window frame.

  • We had a short break.

  • We had a break lasting 11 minutes and 42 seconds.

25.6.2 Cognitive overload

  • First wash the rice, add water, and cook.

  • First wash the rice in repeated cycles, observe particle movement, regulate water volume in detail, and monitor stage transitions continuously.

25.6.3 False precision

  • The trip will take about an hour.

  • The trip will take exactly 61.8 minutes.

  • There were around thirty people there.

  • There were 31.27 people there.

25.6.4 Simpler language being better

  • The road is dangerous.

  • The road presents a layered profile of variable multi-factor hazard exposure.

  • He is very tired.

  • He is experiencing a multi-dimensional decrement in bodily and cognitive energy allocation.

These examples show that overprecision often includes:

  • unnecessary detail

  • bad proportion

  • attention burden

  • pseudo-exactness

  • loss of communicative fit

25.7 Why this matters

To understand overprecision is to understand that semantic maturity is not the same as maximal detail.

Without this idea, a person may chase precision mechanically and produce language that is less useful, less readable, or less honest. One may confuse technical surface with genuine clarity. But higher linguistic control requires a more disciplined question:

Does this extra precision actually improve understanding?

Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes the answer is no.

Overprecision is also essential because many later structures depend on handling it well:

  • structured expression in Volume III will require proportion, not just detail

  • explanation becomes weaker when burdened by unnecessary precision

  • evaluation becomes less trustworthy when false precision enters

  • context determines how much exactness is appropriate

  • good writing often depends on selecting the right level of detail, not the most detail possible

Granular language therefore requires not only the ability to refine meaning, but the judgment to stop refining when refinement stops helping.

25.8 Chapter summary

This chapter introduces overprecision as the failure that occurs when language becomes more exact than the situation meaningfully requires.

Overprecision may appear through irrelevant detail, cognitive overload, false precision, or excessive narrowing where simpler language would work better.

25.8.1 Knowledge Refined

Overprecision is the excess of exactness — the mistake of thinking that more detail always means better communication. It is the necessary counterbalance to the entire volume: granular language is not about maximizing resolution at all times, but about choosing the right resolution for the situation. Overprecision shows that precision has a cost.

The forms of overprecision:

Form Description Example Better Alternative
Irrelevant detail Information that does not serve the purpose The 2.3-mile walk took 37 minutes (when the time does not matter) It took about half an hour
Cognitive overload Too much detail for the listener to process A paragraph describing every feature of a simple object It is a standard office chair
False precision Exact numbers that imply more accuracy than exists The population is 247,893 (from an estimate) About 250,000
Excessive narrowing Overly specific terms where a general one suffices Please hand me the 7-inch serrated bread knife with the wooden handle Please hand me the bread knife

Key insight: Precision is a tool, not a goal. The goal is effective communication. Precision serves that goal up to a point — beyond that point, it becomes noise. Knowing where that point lies is a mark of language maturity.

25.8.2 Must Remember

Concept Definition Why It Matters
More detail ≠ better language Precision must serve the purpose of communication This counters the assumption that this volume might have encouraged — that more resolution is always better
Irrelevant detail Details that do not matter for the context They waste attention and obscure what is important
Cognitive overload Too much information for the listener to absorb The most precise message is one the listener can actually understand
False precision Numbers that look exact but are not reliable Exact-seeming language can be more misleading than honest vagueness
Proportion is key True precision matches the level of detail to the situation This is the final and most important discipline of granular language

To recognize overprecision is to complete the discipline of semantic refinement. Language becomes mature not when it is maximally exact at all times, but when it knows how much exactness the situation can truly bear.
