13  Dependence and Condition

Not every important relation is a direct cause. Some things depend on conditions.

A plant depends on water, light, and soil. A machine requires power. Recovery depends on rest. Good work often depends on time, attention, and the right tools. A door may open only if it is unlocked. A plan may succeed only if enough support is available. In these cases, one thing does not always directly cause another in a simple one-step way. Instead, it makes the other thing possible, supports it, or sets the condition under which it can happen.

This is why dependence and condition matter.

Granular language becomes more precise when it can distinguish direct causation from conditional structure. Sometimes one thing produces another. But sometimes the more important fact is that one thing relies on another, requires another, or can happen only under certain conditions. This is a different kind of relation, and it often gives a more accurate account of how reality works.

13.1 What are dependence and condition?

At the level of ordinary language, dependence means that one thing relies on another, and condition means that something must be true or available for another thing to happen, function, or continue.

We notice dependence and condition through simple patterns such as:

  • depends on

  • requires

  • if and unless

  • enabling conditions

  • necessary and optional conditions

Examples include:

  • growth depends on food and care

  • clear work requires attention

  • a fire can burn only if fuel is present

  • a person improves if rest is sufficient

  • the trip will fail unless the road is open

Dependence is not always direct production.
It is often structured reliance.

13.2 Why dependence and condition matter

Dependence and condition matter because many realities are not best understood as simple one-step causes.

We need to know:

  • what something depends on

  • what something requires

  • what must be present for something else to happen

  • what helps but is not strictly necessary

  • how conditional language increases semantic precision

Without this, language easily becomes too crude. A person may say one thing “caused” another when the relation is better described as support, requirement, or condition. Higher-resolution language becomes stronger when it can say not only what produced a result, but what made the result possible.

13.3 Key distinctions

13.3.1 1. Depends on

Some things rely on other things for support, continuation, or success.

Pattern Description Examples
depends on one thing relies on another health depends on rest, plants depend on water, trust depends on honesty
does not stand alone the thing cannot function well by itself recovery without rest, growth without food, work without tools

Examples:

  • Good health depends on sleep and food.

  • Plants depend on water and light.

  • Learning depends on practice.

  • Safety often depends on careful attention.

This matters because dependence highlights ongoing support, not just one-time causation.

13.3.2 2. Requires

Some conditions are not merely helpful. They are required.

Pattern Description Examples
requires something is necessary for another thing to happen or function breathing requires air, travel requires time, reading requires light
does not require not necessary in the same strict sense a comfortable chair may help reading, but is not always required

Examples:

  • Life requires water.

  • Good work requires attention.

  • The machine requires electricity.

  • Travel requires time and access.

This matters because requires expresses a stronger relation than ordinary help or support.

13.3.3 3. If and unless

Language often marks condition through conditional forms.

Form Description Examples
if under this condition, something happens or becomes possible If it rains, the road becomes wet.
unless without this condition, something will not happen or will fail Unless you rest, you will stay tired.

Examples:

  • If the weather is clear, we will go.

  • If you heat the water, it will boil.

  • Unless the door is unlocked, it will not open.

  • Unless we leave now, we will be late.

This matters because conditional language shows dependence without always claiming direct causation in a narrow sense.

13.3.4 4. Enabling conditions

Some conditions do not force an outcome, but make it possible.

Pattern Description Examples
enabling condition something that allows or supports another thing to occur enough time, open road, working tools, safe environment
blocking condition something that prevents the outcome locked gate, no fuel, poor signal, lack of access

Examples:

  • Good tools enable faster work.

  • A safe space enables recovery.

  • Enough light enables careful reading.

  • Lack of support can block progress.

This matters because reality often works through possibility and constraint, not just through simple production.

13.3.5 5. Necessary vs. optional conditions

Not every condition has the same status.

Type Description Examples
necessary condition must be present for something to happen air for breathing, fuel for fire, water for plant survival
optional condition helpful or improving, but not strictly required music during work, a nicer chair, extra decoration

Examples:

  • Water is necessary for plant life.

  • Sleep is necessary for recovery.

  • Quiet is helpful for study, but not always necessary.

  • A larger table may be useful, but not required.

This matters because semantic precision improves when we distinguish what is essential from what is merely helpful.

13.4 Core patterns

This chapter rests on a few basic patterns.

13.4.1 Basic semantic patterns

  • Some things depend on other things.

  • Some things require certain conditions.

  • Conditional language often uses if and unless.

  • Some conditions enable.

  • Some conditions block.

  • Some conditions are necessary.

  • Some conditions are optional.

13.4.2 Basic language patterns

  • ___ depends on ___.

  • ___ requires ___.

  • If ___, then ___.

  • Unless ___, ___ will not happen.

  • ___ is an enabling condition for ___.

  • ___ is necessary for ___.

  • ___ is helpful, but not necessary.

  • ___ cannot happen without ___.

13.5 Dependence and condition and the movement of this volume

Dependence and condition extend the work of cause and effect.

13.5.1 Cause and effect show production

  • one thing helps bring about another

13.5.2 Dependence and condition show structured reliance

  • one thing needs another to function

  • one outcome becomes possible only under certain conditions

  • one relation may be enabling rather than directly productive

This is an important refinement. Language is no longer asking only, “What caused this?” It is also asking, “What does this depend on?” and “What conditions make this possible?”

13.6 Examples

Here are some clean examples of dependence-and-condition language.

13.6.1 Depends on

  • Good health depends on sleep.

  • Trust depends on honesty.

  • Learning depends on practice.

  • Growth depends on food and care.

13.6.2 Requires

  • The machine requires electricity.

  • Reading requires light.

  • Breathing requires air.

  • Good work requires attention.

13.6.3 If / unless

  • If you add water, the rice will cook.

  • If the road is clear, we can leave.

  • Unless you rest, you will stay tired.

  • Unless the door is unlocked, it will not open.

13.6.4 Enabling conditions

  • Enough time enables careful work.

  • A stable table enables safe cutting.

  • Warmth enables comfort in winter.

  • Clear instructions enable better coordination.

13.6.5 Necessary vs. optional conditions

  • Water is necessary for life.

  • Sleep is necessary for recovery.

  • Music may help, but it is optional.

  • Extra space is useful, but not always necessary.

These examples show that dependence-and-condition language often includes:

  • reliance

  • requirement

  • possibility

  • support

  • constraint

  • necessity

13.7 Why this matters

To understand dependence and condition is to understand that many structures of reality are conditional rather than simply causal.

Without this idea, language remains too narrow. A person may look only for direct causes and miss the deeper network of support, requirement, and enabling conditions that actually shape outcomes. Higher-resolution thought becomes stronger when it can distinguish:

  • what causes

  • what supports

  • what allows

  • what is required

  • what is optional

Dependence and condition are also essential because many later structures depend on them:

  • explanation becomes deeper when conditions are visible

  • planning improves when necessary conditions are identified

  • judgment becomes more realistic when enabling and blocking conditions are noticed

  • systems thinking grows stronger when structured reliance is made explicit

  • semantic precision increases when direct cause is separated from dependency and necessity

Granular language therefore requires more than tracing effects back to causes. It also requires seeing what outcomes stand upon.

13.8 Chapter summary

This chapter introduces dependence and condition as structural relations of support, requirement, and possibility.

Some things are not best understood as directly causing one another. Instead, they depend on certain conditions, require certain supports, and become possible only if enabling conditions are present. Important patterns include depends on, requires, if and unless, enabling conditions, and necessary versus optional conditions.

The central lessons of this chapter are:

  • not all important relations are direct causes

  • some things depend on other things

  • some things require certain conditions

  • if and unless express conditional structure

  • some conditions enable and some block

  • necessary conditions differ from optional ones

To recognize dependence and condition is to move from simple causation toward deeper structural understanding. This is one of the ways language becomes capable of describing not only what produces results, but what makes results possible at all.
