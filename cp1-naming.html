2  Naming

Language begins by selecting.

Before a word can explain, compare, qualify, or organize, it must first pick something out. This is one of the earliest and most basic acts of language. A word does not give us the whole reality of a thing, but it gives attention a target. It says: look here, not everywhere; take this as one item, not as part of an undivided blur. In this sense, naming is the first semantic act by which the world becomes manageable to thought.

This is why naming comes first in granular language.

At the level of ordinary experience, the world is richer than any first description of it. A child may see movement, color, sound, shape, and texture all at once. But language does not begin by expressing all of that complexity. It begins by selecting a unit and attaching a word to it: dog, water, mother, hot, pain, food. Naming reduces the field. It cuts something out from the surrounding flow and gives it a stable point of reference.

2.1 What is naming?

At the level of common use, naming is the act by which language identifies or selects something as an object of attention.

We notice naming through simple realities such as:

  • pointing to something and giving it a word

  • using a word to identify a kind of thing

  • distinguishing one thing from another by name

  • moving from vague perception to stable reference

Examples include:

  • calling a tree tree

  • calling a feeling pain

  • calling a sound music

  • calling a person teacher

  • calling a place home

A name does not contain all knowledge.
But it creates a first handle for thought.

2.2 Why naming matters

Naming matters because thought needs stable points of selection.

We need to know:

  • how language first picks things out

  • that a word can direct attention

  • that naming is not the same as full understanding

  • that some names are broader and others more precise

  • that naming is the beginning of semantic control

Without naming, attention remains too diffuse. We may sense many things, but we cannot easily hold them apart, return to them, compare them, or speak about them clearly. Naming is therefore one of the first ways language transforms perception into usable thought.

2.3 Key distinctions

2.3.1 1. Naming as selection

A name does not describe everything. It selects something.

Aspect Description Examples
selection a word picks out one item, class, or region of experience tree, bird, pain, noise
non-selection experience remains present but linguistically unshaped vague feeling, undifferentiated scene, unclassified sound

Examples:

  • The child points and says dog.

  • We hear a sound and call it music.

  • Someone feels a condition and calls it pain.

  • A moving shape becomes bird once it is named.

This matters because language begins not by exhausting reality, but by selecting from it.

2.3.2 2. A word picks out something

A word functions by referring attention toward something.

Type Description Examples
object name picks out a thing cup, stone, car
event name picks out a happening rain, accident, meeting
state name picks out a condition sleep, silence, pain
role name picks out a social position or function mother, teacher, worker

Examples:

  • Cup picks out an object.

  • Rain picks out an event or condition of weather.

  • Pain picks out an experience or bodily state.

  • Teacher picks out a role.

A word is therefore not only a sound or symbol. It is a selector of attention.

2.3.3 3. Not all names are equally precise

Some names are broad, while others pick out something more narrowly.

Type Description Examples
broad name covers a wide range animal, food, tool, plant
narrower name covers a smaller range dog, bread, hammer, tree
highly specific name picks out a very narrow kind or item Labrador, sourdough bread, claw hammer, oak tree

Examples:

  • Animal is less precise than dog.

  • Food is less precise than rice.

  • Tool is less precise than knife.

  • Tree is less precise than pine tree.

This matters because naming does not happen at only one level. A name may grip the world loosely or tightly.

2.3.4 4. Common names and specific names

Ordinary language often moves between common naming and more specific naming.

Type Description Examples
common name an everyday general label used by many people tree, car, bird, teacher
specific name a narrower or more exact label oak, sedan, sparrow, math teacher

Examples:

  • Tree is a common name; oak is more specific.

  • Car is a common name; truck or sedan is more specific.

  • Bird is a common name; eagle is more specific.

  • Teacher is a common name; history teacher is more specific.

Common names are useful because they simplify.
Specific names are useful because they refine.

2.4 Core patterns

This chapter rests on a few basic patterns.

2.4.1 Basic semantic patterns

  • Language begins by naming.

  • Naming selects something from the world.

  • A word picks out an object, event, state, role, or kind.

  • Naming does not equal full understanding.

  • Some names are broad.

  • Some names are specific.

  • Greater specificity often increases semantic resolution.

2.4.2 Basic language patterns

  • This is a ___.

  • That is called ___.

  • The word ___ picks out ___.

  • ___ is a broad name.

  • ___ is a more specific name than ___.

  • We call this ___.

  • That name is too broad / more precise.

2.5 Naming and the larger movement of this volume

Naming is the beginning of granularity, but not its end.

2.5.1 Naming comes before distinction

  • We often need a name before we can compare or refine.

  • A thing must first be selected before it can be divided into subtypes.

2.5.2 Naming comes before qualification

  • We first say tree, and only then tall tree, young tree, dying tree.

  • We first say pain, and only then sharp pain, dull pain, chronic pain.

2.5.3 Naming comes before relation

  • We first identify the objects before we can say how they relate.

  • We name parent and child before describing their relation.

This is why naming is the entry point into higher resolution. It gives language something to work on.

2.6 Examples

Here are some clean examples of naming in use.

2.6.1 Basic naming

  • This is a dog.

  • That is water.

  • This feeling is pain.

  • That sound is music.

  • This place is home.

2.6.2 Broad vs. specific names

  • Animal is broad; dog is more specific.

  • Fruit is broad; apple is more specific.

  • Vehicle is broad; bicycle is more specific.

  • Teacher is broad; science teacher is more specific.

2.6.3 Naming as selection

  • We call this tool a hammer.

  • She called the feeling fear.

  • He named the problem fatigue.

  • The child learned the word bird and began to notice birds more clearly.

These examples show that naming language often includes:

  • selection

  • reference

  • classification

  • level of specificity

  • first semantic control

2.7 Why this matters

To understand naming is to understand the first cut language makes into the world.

Without naming, thought remains too loose. We may still perceive, feel, or react, but we cannot easily stabilize what we are attending to. Naming makes return possible. Once something has a name, it can be recalled, contrasted, refined, questioned, and shared with others.

Naming is also essential because many later structures depend on it:

  • categories depend on names

  • boundaries depend on named distinctions

  • qualification depends on named objects or conditions

  • relation depends on named entities

  • higher semantic precision begins with stable selection

But naming also has a limit. A name may establish an object without yet revealing its full structure. This is why naming is necessary, but not enough.

2.8 Chapter summary

This chapter introduces naming as the first semantic act by which language establishes objects of attention.

Language begins by selecting. A word picks out something from the world and gives thought a stable handle. But not all names are equally precise. Some are broad, and some are more specific. Common names simplify; specific names refine.

The central lessons of this chapter are:

  • language begins through naming

  • naming is a form of selection

  • a word picks out something

  • naming is not the same as full understanding

  • some names are broader than others

  • common names and specific names differ in resolution

To recognize naming is to recognize the first way language cuts the world into thinkable form. It is the beginning of semantic resolution, because before we can distinguish more finely, we must first know what has been selected at all.
