9  Degree

Many attributes are not simply present or absent. They vary by degree.

A person may be weak or strong, but also somewhat weak, fairly strong, or extremely strong. Water may be warm, hot, or only slightly warm. A room may be small, large, or somewhere in between. A feeling may be mild, strong, or overwhelming. In ordinary language, many qualities do not work like switches that are either on or off. They work more like scales.

This is why degree matters.

After language has learned to name objects and describe attributes, it must take another step: it must learn to place qualities along a range. This is one of the main ways semantic resolution increases. A simple attribute such as warm tells us something, but degree tells us more. It allows language to say not only what kind of quality is present, but how much of it is present.

9.1 What is degree?

At the level of ordinary language, degree is the amount, intensity, or level of a quality.

We notice degree through simple patterns such as:

  • weak and strong

  • warm and hot

  • small and large

  • slightly, very, and extremely

  • scalar meaning in general

Examples include:

  • slightly tired

  • very cold

  • extremely loud

  • somewhat weak

  • highly useful

Degree adds scale to attributes.
It turns flat description into graded description.

9.2 Why degree matters

Degree matters because ordinary reality is rarely all-or-nothing.

We need to know:

  • that many qualities come in levels

  • that one thing may be more or less of a quality than another

  • that language often needs intensifiers or scalar contrasts

  • that resolution improves when degree is noticed clearly

Without degree, description becomes too blunt. We may know that a room is warm, but not whether it is slightly warm or extremely hot. We may know that a person is tired, but not whether the tiredness is mild or severe. Degree gives language control over intensity, scale, and threshold.

9.3 Key distinctions

9.3.1 1. Weak and strong

Some qualities vary in force or power.

Degree Description Examples
weak low in force, intensity, or capacity weak muscles, weak signal, weak argument
strong high in force, intensity, or capacity strong body, strong smell, strong support

Examples:

  • His legs feel weak.

  • She has a strong grip.

  • The signal is weak here.

  • That smell is very strong.

This matters because many bodily, physical, and abstract qualities are understood through strength of degree.

9.3.2 2. Warm and hot

Temperature is a clear example of scalar meaning.

Degree Description Examples
warm moderately high temperature warm room, warm water
hot higher temperature hot tea, hot pan, hot weather

Examples:

  • The water is warm.

  • The tea is hot.

  • The room is slightly warm.

  • The pan is too hot to touch.

This matters because a shift in degree can change comfort, safety, and practical response.

9.3.3 3. Small and large

Many size-related attributes also work by degree.

Degree Description Examples
small lower in size or scale small room, small bag, small dog
large higher in size or scale large building, large box, large crowd

Examples:

  • The box is small.

  • The house is large.

  • The difference is very small.

  • The crowd became extremely large.

This matters because size is often not absolute. It is judged relative to expectation, use, or comparison.

9.3.4 4. Slightly, very, extremely

Language often marks degree through modifiers.

Modifier Description Examples
slightly low degree slightly warm, slightly tired, slightly wet
very high degree very cold, very useful, very large
extremely very high degree extremely hot, extremely difficult, extremely tired

Examples:

  • I am slightly tired.

  • The water is very cold.

  • The room is extremely quiet.

  • She was slightly worried at first.

These words matter because they help language measure intensity without using exact numbers.

9.3.5 5. Scalar meaning

Some words naturally imply a scale rather than a simple yes-or-no state.

Scalar quality Lower range Higher range
temperature cool warm / hot
strength weak strong
size small large
speed slow fast
intensity slight extreme

Examples:

  • The noise became louder.

  • He is stronger than before.

  • The road feels narrower here.

  • The pain is getting worse.

Scalar meaning matters because it allows language to track increase, decrease, threshold, and comparison more precisely.

9.4 Core patterns

This chapter rests on a few basic patterns.

9.4.1 Basic semantic patterns

  • Many attributes vary by degree.

  • Degree adds scale to description.

  • Some qualities are low in intensity.

  • Some qualities are high in intensity.

  • Degree can be marked by contrast words or modifiers.

  • Scalar meaning makes description more precise.

9.4.2 Basic language patterns

  • ___ is weak / strong.

  • ___ is warm / hot.

  • ___ is small / large.

  • ___ is slightly ___.

  • ___ is very ___.

  • ___ is extremely ___.

  • The degree of ___ is high / low.

  • ___ became more / less ___.

9.5 Degree and the movement of this volume

Degree extends the work of attributes.

9.5.1 Attributes name the quality

  • a room is warm

  • a bag is heavy

  • a person is tired

9.5.2 Degree places the quality on a scale

  • slightly warm

  • very heavy

  • extremely tired

This is a major refinement. Language is no longer just attaching qualities to objects. It is beginning to calibrate those qualities.

9.6 Examples

Here are some clean examples of degree language.

9.6.1 Bodily condition

  • I feel slightly tired.

  • He is very weak today.

  • She became extremely tired after the trip.

  • My back pain is mild, not severe.

9.6.2 Temperature

  • The water is warm.

  • The tea is very hot.

  • The air is slightly cold.

  • The room became too warm.

9.6.3 Size

  • The bag is small.

  • The building is very large.

  • The difference is quite small.

  • That object is extremely large for this space.

9.6.4 General intensity

  • The sound is very loud.

  • The light is slightly dim.

  • The task is extremely difficult.

  • Her voice was a little soft.

These examples show that degree language often includes:

  • intensity

  • scale

  • modifiers

  • thresholds

  • increasing precision

9.7 Why this matters

To understand degree is to understand that many qualities are gradual rather than binary.

Without this idea, language stays too rough. A person may say that something is hot, tired, difficult, large, or dangerous without showing how much. Degree allows thought to become more measured. It makes room for low, medium, and high intensity; for mild, moderate, and severe condition; for slight change and major change.

Degree is also essential because many later structures depend on it:

  • measure will make some degrees more exact

  • comparison will often compare degrees across objects

  • vagueness and thresholds will later show where degree becomes hard to fix precisely

  • better judgment often depends on noticing not just quality, but intensity

Granular language therefore requires more than naming qualities. It requires scaling them.

9.8 Chapter summary

This chapter introduces degree as the scalar dimension of many attributes.

Many qualities are not simply present or absent. They vary by amount, force, intensity, or level. Common examples include weak and strong, warm and hot, small and large, and modifiers such as slightly, very, and extremely.

9.8.1 Knowledge Refined

Degree transforms description from binary (present/absent) to scalar (more/less). Without degree, language can only say whether a quality exists; with degree, it can say how much — which is essential for precision. The same attribute (e.g., temperature) can be expressed at multiple levels of intensity.

The structure of scalar meaning:

Intensity Level Modifier Example Attribute Example Resolution Added
Low slightly, a bit slightly warm, a bit soft Minimal presence
Medium rather, fairly rather warm, fairly soft Moderate presence
High very, extremely very warm, extremely soft Strong presence
Extreme absolutely, completely absolutely freezing Near boundary of scale

Key insight: Most qualities in ordinary language are not absolute — they admit degrees. Learning to mark degree (with modifiers, comparatives, or scale words) is one of the most powerful ways to increase semantic resolution.

9.8.2 Must Remember

Concept Definition Why It Matters
Attributes vary by degree Most qualities exist on a continuum, not as binary opposites Without degree, language flattens the richness of experience
Modifiers mark intensity Words like slightly, very, and extremely calibrate degree They make description more precise by locating a point on a scale
Scalar meaning is pervasive Warm/cold, tall/short, fast/slow all have scalar structure Recognizing scalarity helps you avoid false dichotomies
Degree increases resolution From “warm” to “slightly warm” is a gain in precision The difference between flat and graded description is often the difference between vague and useful

To recognize degree is to move from flat description toward graded description. This is one of the most important ways language increases the resolution of thought.
