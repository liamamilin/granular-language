6  Levels of Specificity

The same thing can be said broadly or narrowly.

A knife can be called a thing, a tool, or a knife. An apple can be called food, fruit, or apple. A chair can be called an object, a piece of furniture, or a chair. In each case, the world has not changed, but the level of semantic resolution has changed. Language can grip the same reality loosely or tightly, at a wider level or a narrower one.

This is why levels of specificity matter.

After naming, grouping, and examining category boundaries, language must learn another discipline: how wide or narrow a term is. A broad term gives reach. A narrow term gives detail. Both are useful, but they do not do the same work. Granular language becomes stronger when it can move deliberately between levels rather than speaking at only one fixed level of generality.

6.1 What is specificity?

At the level of ordinary language, specificity is the degree to which a word or expression picks out something broadly or narrowly.

We notice specificity through simple realities such as:

  • using a broad term or a narrow term

  • moving from general class to smaller subtype

  • describing the same thing at different semantic levels

  • increasing resolution by narrowing the label

Examples include:

  • thing → tool → knife

  • food → fruit → apple

  • animal → bird → sparrow

  • vehicle → car → taxi

Specificity does not create a new object.
It changes how finely the object is selected in language.

6.2 Why specificity matters

Specificity matters because understanding often depends on choosing the right level of detail.

We need to know:

  • when a broad term is enough

  • when a narrower term is better

  • how semantic resolution increases through narrower naming

  • that the same thing can be described at several levels

Without this understanding, language easily becomes either too vague or too narrow.

  • If it stays too broad, important distinctions disappear.

  • If it becomes narrow too early, communication may become unnecessarily heavy or precise.

Granular language improves when it can adjust the level of specificity to the task.

6.3 Key distinctions

6.3.1 1. The same thing at multiple levels

One thing may be named at different semantic heights.

Broad level Middle level Narrow level
thing tool knife
food fruit apple
animal bird sparrow
object furniture chair

Examples:

  • This is a thing. More specifically, it is a tool. More specifically still, it is a knife.

  • This is food. More specifically, it is fruit. More specifically still, it is an apple.

  • That is an animal. More specifically, it is a bird. Even more specifically, it is a sparrow.

This matters because specificity is often layered rather than absolute.

6.3.2 2. Broad term vs. narrow term

Some terms cover a wide range, while others select a smaller range.

Type Description Examples
broad term covers many different items thing, food, animal, vehicle
narrow term covers fewer items more exactly knife, apple, sparrow, taxi

Examples:

  • Tool is broader than knife.

  • Fruit is broader than apple.

  • Vehicle is broader than car.

  • Animal is broader than bird.

Broad terms simplify.
Narrow terms refine.

6.3.3 3. Specificity as resolution

Greater specificity usually means higher semantic resolution.

Lower resolution Higher resolution
thing knife
food apple
animal sparrow
clothing winter coat

Examples:

  • Thing gives very low resolution.

  • Tool gives more resolution.

  • Knife gives still more resolution.

  • Kitchen knife gives even greater resolution.

This matters because specificity is one of the main ways language becomes more precise without yet adding many descriptive attributes.

6.3.4 4. Choosing the right level

Higher specificity is not always better in every situation.

Situation Better level Example
broad overview broader term We need food.
practical selection narrower term We need apples.
very precise context highly specific term We need green apples for this recipe.

Examples:

  • In a general discussion, food may be enough.

  • In shopping, fruit may be more helpful.

  • In cooking, apple may be better.

  • In a recipe, green apple may be best.

This matters because semantic control includes knowing when to stop narrowing.

6.4 Core patterns

This chapter rests on a few basic patterns.

6.4.1 Basic semantic patterns

  • The same thing can be named at different levels.

  • Some terms are broad.

  • Some terms are narrow.

  • Narrower terms usually increase resolution.

  • Broader terms simplify.

  • Good language chooses the right level for the context.

6.4.2 Basic language patterns

  • ___ is a broad term.

  • ___ is a narrower term than ___.

  • This is a kind of ___.

  • More specifically, it is ___.

  • At a broad level, this is ___.

  • At a narrower level, this is ___.

  • We need a more specific word.

6.5 Specificity and the movement of this volume

Levels of specificity complete the first major block of this volume.

6.5.1 Naming selects

  • A word first picks something out.

6.5.2 Categories group

  • The selected item is placed into a class.

6.5.3 Boundaries test the class

  • We ask where the class begins and ends.

6.5.4 Specificity adjusts the resolution

  • We move between broad and narrow levels within the same semantic field.

This is an important transition. Up to this point, language has learned to select, group, and examine boundaries. With specificity, it now learns to control semantic scale more deliberately.

6.6 Examples

Here are some clean examples of specificity in use.

6.6.1 Basic semantic ladders

  • thing → tool → knife

  • food → fruit → apple

  • animal → bird → sparrow

  • object → furniture → chair

6.6.2 Broad vs. narrow examples

  • Thing is broader than tool.

  • Tool is broader than knife.

  • Food is broader than fruit.

  • Fruit is broader than apple.

6.6.3 Resolution in context

  • We need food.

  • More specifically, we need fruit.

  • More specifically still, we need apples.

  • He used a tool.

  • More specifically, he used a knife.

  • Even more specifically, he used a kitchen knife.

These examples show that specificity language often includes:

  • semantic layering

  • broader and narrower terms

  • resolution shifts

  • contextual choice

  • controlled refinement

6.7 Why this matters

To understand specificity is to understand that semantic resolution has levels.

Without this idea, language often becomes stuck at one layer. A person may speak too broadly and lose useful detail, or speak too narrowly and overload a simple situation. Higher control comes from being able to move between levels deliberately.

Specificity is also essential because many later structures depend on it:

  • attributes will refine already selected objects

  • degree will sharpen already named qualities

  • comparison becomes stronger when items are named at the right level

  • context often determines how specific language should be

  • semantic precision grows not only by adding words, but by choosing the right semantic level

Granular language therefore requires more than naming and grouping. It requires command over semantic scale.

6.8 Chapter summary

This chapter introduces levels of specificity as one of the main ways language increases resolution.

The same thing can be described broadly or narrowly. A knife may be called a thing, a tool, or a knife. An apple may be called food, fruit, or apple. Broad terms simplify, while narrow terms refine.

6.8.1 Knowledge Refined

Specificity is the dimension of semantic scale. Unlike categorization (which groups different items under one class), specificity moves along a single chain from general to particular for the same referent. This gives language a zoom lens: the broad end gives orientation, the narrow end gives precision.

The specificity hierarchy:

Level Name Use Case Example
Superordinate Broad class First orientation, context setting animal, tool, food
Basic level Everyday reference Normal communication dog, hammer, apple
Subordinate Specific subtype Precision, expert context Labrador, claw hammer, Granny Smith

Key insight: Moving along the specificity scale does not change the object — it changes the resolution at which the object is described. A skilled communicator knows which level the situation demands and adjusts accordingly.

6.8.2 Must Remember

Concept Definition Why It Matters
Same referent, different levels The same object can be named at multiple levels of specificity This gives language flexibility — you can zoom in or out depending on need
Broad terms simplify General words cover more ground with less detail Use when the audience needs orientation, not precision
Narrow terms sharpen Specific words convey finer distinctions Use when accuracy matters more than generality
Choosing the right level Good language selects the appropriate specificity for the context A key skill of clear communication is knowing when broad is enough and when narrow is required

To recognize levels of specificity is to gain control over semantic scale. Language becomes more precise not only by adding description, but by knowing how broadly or narrowly to name what is already there.
