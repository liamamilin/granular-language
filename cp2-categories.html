3  Categories

After naming, language begins to group.

A single name may pick out one thing, but thought does not remain at the level of isolated items for long. Once objects, actions, and conditions are named, the mind begins to place them into classes. A dog is not only this dog; it is also an animal. A rose is not only this flower; it is also a plant. A chair is not only one object in a room; it belongs to a wider class of things made for sitting. In this way, language moves from selection to grouping.

This is why categories come immediately after naming.

Naming gives thought a first handle. Categorization gives it structure. It allows many different things to be treated together under a larger heading. Without this, the world would remain too scattered for efficient understanding. We would know many separate items, but not the larger classes that connect them. Categories reduce complexity by gathering multiple instances into a smaller number of intelligible groups.

3.1 What is a category?

At the level of ordinary language, a category is a group into which similar things are placed.

We notice categories through simple realities such as:

  • grouping similar things together

  • placing one thing under a larger class

  • moving from particular items to general kinds

  • using broader words to organize many narrower ones

Examples include:

  • placing dogs, birds, and fish under animal

  • placing roses, trees, and grass under plant

  • placing cups, tables, and doors under object

  • placing running, eating, and speaking under action

A category does not name only one thing.
It gathers many things under one larger heading.

3.2 Why categories matter

Categories matter because the world contains too many separate items to handle one by one.

We need to know:

  • how similar things are grouped

  • how broader classes organize narrower items

  • that some categories are wider than others

  • why classification helps thought move more quickly and clearly

Without categories, language would become overloaded with isolated names. We might know dog, cat, bird, cow, and fish, but fail to see that all of them can be grouped as animals. Categorization gives economy to thought. It simplifies reality without completely losing contact with it.

3.3 Key distinctions

3.3.1 1. Category as grouping

A category brings several things together under one class.

Aspect Description Examples
grouping placing similar items together dog, cat, horse → animal
individual item one member of a category one dog, one cat, one horse

Examples:

  • A dog is an animal.

  • A rose is a plant.

  • A cup is an object.

  • Running is an action.

This matters because categorization allows language to move from many separate items to a smaller number of classes.

3.3.2 2. Common basic categories

Ordinary language often begins with a few broad and powerful classes.

Category What it groups Examples
animal living creatures that move and live as animals dog, bird, fish, cow
plant living things that grow as plants tree, flower, grass, rice
object non-living things treated as items chair, stone, cup, phone
action doings, acts, or processes walking, eating, speaking, writing

Examples:

  • A bird is an animal.

  • A tree is a plant.

  • A table is an object.

  • Sleeping is an action or activity.

These categories are powerful because they provide broad first-level organization.

3.3.3 3. Broad classes and narrow classes

Not all categories operate at the same level.

Type Description Examples
broad class a wide category containing many kinds animal, plant, object, action
narrow class a smaller category within a broader one bird, tree, tool, running

Examples:

  • Animal is broader than bird.

  • Plant is broader than tree.

  • Object is broader than tool.

  • Action is broader than running.

This matters because categorization is layered. A thing can belong to both a wider and a narrower class.

3.3.4 4. Why categorization simplifies reality

Reality is more detailed than any first classification. Categories simplify by reducing many differences into usable groups.

Function Description Examples
simplification reducing complexity into broader classes grouping many animals under one word
loss of detail some differences disappear at the broader level dog and fish are both animals, though very different

Examples:

  • Calling both dogs and birds animals simplifies reality.

  • Calling chairs and tables objects simplifies reality.

  • Calling walking and running actions simplifies reality.

This matters because categorization is useful precisely because it does not keep every difference in view at once.

3.4 Core patterns

This chapter rests on a few basic patterns.

3.4.1 Basic semantic patterns

  • After naming, language groups.

  • A category gathers similar items.

  • One thing may belong to a larger class.

  • Some categories are broad.

  • Some categories are narrow.

  • Categorization simplifies reality.

3.4.2 Basic language patterns

  • ___ is a kind of ___.

  • ___ belongs to the category of ___.

  • ___ and ___ are both ___.

  • ___ is a broad class.

  • ___ is a narrower class within ___.

  • We group ___ under ___.

3.5 Categories and the movement of this volume

Categories are the next step after naming, but they still remain relatively coarse.

3.5.1 Naming selects

  • A word picks out one thing or kind.

  • Dog names a kind of animal.

3.5.2 Categorization groups

  • Animal groups dog, cat, bird, and fish.

  • The mind moves from one named thing to a larger class.

3.5.3 Higher resolution comes later

  • After categories, language will need boundaries, attributes, degree, relation, and perspective.

  • Categories simplify reality, but do not yet describe it finely.

This is why categories are necessary but limited. They give structure, but not full precision.

3.6 Examples

Here are some clean examples of categories in use.

3.6.1 Basic category statements

  • A dog is an animal.

  • A tree is a plant.

  • A cup is an object.

  • Running is an action.

3.6.2 Broad and narrow class statements

  • A bird is a kind of animal.

  • A rose is a kind of plant.

  • A hammer is a kind of object or tool.

  • Walking is a kind of action.

3.6.3 Category grouping

  • Dogs, cats, and horses belong to the category of animal.

  • Trees, flowers, and grass belong to the category of plant.

  • Chairs, tables, and phones belong to the category of object.

  • Eating, speaking, and working belong to the category of action.

These examples show that category language often includes:

  • grouping

  • class membership

  • broad and narrow levels

  • simplification

  • first structural organization

3.7 Why this matters

To understand categories is to understand one of the earliest ways language organizes the world.

Without categories, thought becomes too scattered. We may know many names, but we cannot easily compress reality into usable groupings. Categories make memory, comparison, and communication easier. They allow us to move from isolated items to broader structures.

Categories are also essential because many later structures depend on them:

  • boundaries depend on categories

  • specificity depends on movement between broad and narrow classes

  • qualification depends on already grouped objects

  • comparison often begins within or across categories

  • semantic refinement begins by seeing how categories simplify and where they become too rough

But categories also introduce a tension: they simplify reality by reducing detail. That simplification is powerful, but it also prepares the need for finer distinctions.

3.8 Chapter summary

This chapter introduces categories as the grouping structures that follow naming.

After naming, language places things into classes. A category gathers similar items under a larger heading. Common examples include animal, plant, object, and action. Some categories are broad, while others are narrower. Categorization simplifies reality and makes thought more manageable.

The central lessons of this chapter are:

  • naming is followed by grouping

  • a category gathers similar things

  • one thing may belong to a broader class

  • some classes are broad and some are narrow

  • categorization simplifies reality

  • simplification is useful, but it also reduces detail

To recognize categories is to recognize the second major step in semantic resolution. Language not only selects things; it also groups them, making the world easier to organize, though still not yet fully precise.
